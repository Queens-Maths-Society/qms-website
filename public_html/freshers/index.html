Queens' Mathematics Freshers' Guide

Haipei Jiang, Anya Khurana, Tada Boyle
July 2025

1 Welcome to Queens'!

First of all, we would just like to extend a massive congratulations for making it here. You've endured writing personal statements, made it through interviews and most scary of all you've survived STEP. You should be proud that you've made it, and will soon be studying the best course, at the best university in the world, at the best college in Cambridge. We've included our contact info in this email (and will create a Whatsapp group too), so feel free to email/message us with any questions you might have. We are your subject contacts, which means we are a group of 2nd and 3rd year mathmos (Cambridge slang for mathematician) at Queens', who are here to offer you advice on all things maths related. We are more than happy to answer any questions before term starts and we're looking forward to meeting you all in Freshers' week. Here are some general tips on how to survive your first year.

2 Structure of First Year

Your first year will start off with freshers' week, meeting new people and going to lots of fun events (don't worry if it all seems like a lot at this stage - that's perfectly normal). Then for your first two terms (Michaelmas and Lent), there will be two lectures each morning, Monday to Saturday. We promise Saturday lectures are not as bad as they seem, especially when there's Queens' brunch right after.

Beware: the first week or so of courses will be mostly revision of A-level material. But, after this, you'll quickly realise that the courses are hard. This is perfectly normal and there is A LOT of material to pack into 50 minutes, so don't panic - this is why you're advised to go over content after lectures. For these two terms, you'll study 4 courses a term. Each first-year course con- sists of 24 lectures, 4 example sheets and a supervision and example class for each sheet (more about these later). Look out for a booklet (it is online at https://www.maths.cam.ac.uk/undergrad/files/schedules.pdf, but is often given to you at the beginning of term) of the schedules and information of the courses.

Your third term (Easter term) will be mainly revision for exams. There will be a couple 12- lecture courses being taught at this time, however they aren't examined until the second year (more info closer to the time). Around week 6 of Easter term you'll have your exams. Each paper consists of exam questions based on one pure and one applied lecture course, and last 3 hours. You'll be feeling smug as a mathmo, since our exams finish quite early compared to all other STEM courses. Summer, with no work, is the best time of the year to be in Cambridge - you can properly enjoy Cambridge in May week (weirdly, this week is not in May), so make the most of it!

2.1 Michaelmas Courses

  • Differential Equations
    • Starts by building on A-Level material, but then there are a lot of new concepts.
    • Sketching phase portraits; logistic map bifurcation; multivariate functions
  • Groups
    • Pure course with a lot of new concepts if you haven't met them before.
    • First isomorphism theorem; orthogonal groups.
  • Numbers and Sets
    • A bit of an introduction to university-style maths, with interesting example sheet questions.
    • Fermat's little theorem; Schöder-Bernstein theorem; there are uncountably many transcendental numbers
  • Vectors and Matrices
    • Also building on A-Level
    • Rank-nullity; eigenvectors and eigenspaces; Cayley-Hamilton theorem

2.2 Lent Courses

  • Analysis I
    • Another course with lots of proofs, and interesting example sheet questions.
    • Mean value theorem; Thomae's function; continuous functions are integrable
  • Dynamics and Relativity
    • Applied course with a fair amount of mechanics/physics
    • Rotating frames; constant acceleration case for the twin paradox; Minkowski space
  • Probability
    • Taught as a pure course so quite a bit of framework
    • Random walks; branching processes; central limit theorem
  • Vector Calculus
    • A nice introduction to a tool which will become very important in later applied courses
    • Curvilinear coordinates (a coordinate system with curved coordinate lines); integral theorems; Poisson and Laplace equations; tensors

Note: it's very easy to go into the year thinking “I'm only going to enjoy the pure courses” or “The applied courses are the only good ones”, but in fact people often find themselves enjoying courses that, based on first appearances, they'd never even have tried - so go into the year with an open mind. If you give them a chance, each course can actually be very enjoyable in its own way.

3 Preparation

Your DoS (Director of Studies) will be Professor Julia Gog.

You will be sent some work before you come to Cambridge - try and get as much of this work done before Freshers' Week, since it's very hectic, and you won't want to miss out on other things. Your future self will definitely be grateful if you have your work ready to hand in. A head start will certainly be useful! That said, the example sheets are a good way to get used to working with other mathmos... and to get to know them as you bond over the shared trauma.

You also need to remember to account for time spent writing-up solutions. Often writing up a solution properly will take just as long as solving the problems.

There are a lot of admin tasks that you will need to do to get set up in Cambridge once you arrive: registering with a GP, updating your address, unpacking, sorting out bank accounts and phone numbers (especially for international students), and plenty of paperwork.

4 How to learn maths once you're here

There are four main parts to learning maths here: Lectures, Example Sheets, Example Classes and Supervisions.

4.1 Lectures

4.1.1 In-Person Lectures

All first-year lectures will be in the Babbage Lecture Theatre (located on the New Museums Site, about a 5-10 minute walk from Queens'). It is usually a tradition for the subject contacts to walk the Freshers to the lecture theatre when they meet in Freshers' Week (we'll contact you about this nearer the time).

Working out where to sit is often your first dilemma in a lecture hall - I would stick together and sit with some other Queens' mathmos as this can make lectures more fun, but don't hesitate to sit with and get to know mathmos from other colleges too.

Officially, 9am lectures start at 9:05am and finish at 9:55 then lectures starting at 10am or 11am start at 10:05am or 11:05am, respectively. This means you have a 10 minute break between lectures! (This can be a useful time to discuss any bits of the lecture with your fellow mathmos that you weren't sure on, or to just relax or meet new people).

Lectures consist of an expert in that area talking to you and writing down the notes, so please be quiet during the lecture so everyone can hear. Despite this, lecturers are human so will make occasional mistakes - if you spot a mistake don't be afraid to speak up.

The most common way to take lecture notes is to write down exactly what the lecturer does, and then annotate this with comments/clarifications afterwards. Pen and paper or an iPad are probably the best way to take down your lecture notes - I'd advise against trying to LiveTex notes, or trying to Latex them afterwards to make them look pretty unless you are pretty confident and speedy at typing maths up. But be aware that you'll need to get used to being able to hand-write maths at speed come exam season.

4.1.2 On-line Lectures

Since COVID, lectures are now almost always recorded. However, these are only up for a few days after lectures, so treat these as a backup in case you're ill/can't attend the lecture for some reason. Don't end up relying on them as an alternative to going to lectures - going to lectures is definitely the best way to learn, it adds some structure to your day and gives you an opportunity to meet other mathmos and make friends from other colleges. If you do have to watch them online for some reason, try to make sure you watch the lectures the day they come out, take notes, sit at a desk and avoid distractions - exactly as you would do in the lecture theatre.

If for any reason you end up needing access to lecture recordings after they are taken down, talk to your DoS - they will be able to restore your access to them.

4.1.3 Lecture Resources

Generally, the lecturer will provide online notes, but beware. These notes are really to supplement the lecture (and can be useful to cross-check your own notes against, in case you or the lecturer has missed something), so it's still important to turn up to the lectures in person (or online if you have to), as the lecturers will give out extra remarks and wisdom which they won't write down. Often previous lecturers or past students publish their notes too. However, courses change over time, so use these with caution. The lectures are always the best primary source of information! These notes do come in handy if the lecturer was unclear and you want an alternative explanation, but be very careful of learning definitions from these notes. You will work out what works best for you in a few weeks' time. Below are some resources that we have found useful:

  • Kevin's Notes (Current Queens' 2nd Year!! The best notes in our unbiased opinion)
  • Dexter's notes (Past student)
  • Dr Gareth Taylor (the legendary Cambridge Supervisor)'s collection of resources
  • Prof. David Tong (especially useful for building intuition in physics-y courses)

Look out, too, for more resources on Moodle (the university's online learning environment), when you gain access.

4.2 Example Sheets

This is where most of the learning happens, along with where most of your maths time will be spent! You will receive one sheet per (roughly) 6 lectures of each course, which works out as two sheets each week. Usually they consist of 10-15 questions. They can be enjoyable, infuriating, or even both, but are nonetheless always highly rewarding. It is recommended to spend about 10-12 hours per sheet, so don't be surprised if you end up spending a lot of time on a single question (this is very different to A-Level, where you're probably used to solving questions in minutes rather than hours).

Also, it is helpful to keep revisiting the problem sheet over the course of a week to give yourself a chance to really think about each problem. Starting the problems as early as possible will make your life a lot easier, and a lot less stressful too! You will also find that the metric for “success” in a sheet is a bit different - for getting the most out of the sheets, the main thing is to have had a decent think about all the questions, rather than to necessarily have completed each and every question. We would recommend prioritising your understanding, so if you notice there is something from lectures you don't understand, even if it is not in the example sheet, try to spend some time thinking about it.

If you get stuck, it is best to ask a supervisor for a hint. It is also useful to talk to other mathmos or work together on difficult questions... but be careful not to spoil the questions for each other by giving too big hints. You will find out quite quickly how to give good hints, and a good hint will look different for different people. Knowing, valuing, and learning to work with other ways of thinking is an important aspect of getting to know your cohort and of your time in Cambridge.

If you can't do a question then just write down your ideas, and anything you've tried and move on (you can always come back to the question later if you have time) to make sure you don't spend hours on one question and therefore neglect half a sheet. As long as you spend enough time thinking about a question you will get what you need to out of it come the supervision.

When you haven't been able to make any progress on a question, your supervisor will be much happier if you hand in a few sentences explaining what confused you and what you tried to do, rather than just ignoring that question entirely. Also bear in mind that examples sheets are not tests, so there is no point in handing in answers that you don't understand, just to impress your supervisor - they'd much rather see what you know and don't know, and your supervisions will be a lot more useful in this case too.

Often examples sheets contain ‘starred' questions. These can be thought of as extension questions. You can try these once you've attempted the rest of the sheet, as these questions can end up taking a lot of time. These questions are where it is especially useful to talk to your fellow mathmos - talking through the ideas you've each had will yield something useful, and you might even solve it together. However, it's important to remember that these questions are intended to be difficult and there is no reason to worry at all if you can't get the answer! It can be nice to read through and just have a go at the question, and you can ask your supervisor about it in the supervision if you found it interesting.

4.3 Examples Classes

This is where Queens' stands out. For each example sheet, the supervisor goes though problems that many people struggled with in a class with all the Queens' mathmos together. This is probably the best time to ask general questions about the lecture content, particularly if there's a number of you who are unsure on something. These classes may happen in person or over a video call. This frees up a lot of time in supervisions, meaning there is plenty of time to ask your specific questions.

4.4 Supervisions

This is one of the main benefits of studying at Cambridge. Supos (Cambridge slang for supervisions) are one-hour slots with just you, your one or two supo partners and your supervisor. These have been both in-person and on-line at different times over the last year. The supervisors are there to clarify anything you didn't understand on the sheet, and will sometimes have some more questions to go through if there is time remaining after discussing the sheet. It is a very good idea to meet up with your supo partner beforehand to decide on which questions to discuss, and if there is anything specific to ask the supervisor about. Some supervisors will go though the entire sheet, but some will let you decide what to talk about, so make sure you look through your work after it's been marked! Also if you don't understand a concept after they've explained it once, ASK FOR IT TO BE EXPLAINED AGAIN. Maths is hard, so there's no shame in not understanding everything perfectly first time. If you use supervisions properly, then they are invaluable - they are really the crown jewel in the Cambridge teaching system.

5 Essential Skills

5.1 Make Use of your Resources

Talking to your fellow mathmos is a great resource. Discussing and checking lecture content and work amongst yourselves is really useful. In fact, this is perhaps your best resource, so make sure to use it! If you are ever stuck on a problem, or can't quite understand a concept DO NOT be afraid to ask your supervisors if a clarification is needed. They are there to support you and will always prefer if you email them with a question than hand in no work because you got stuck on the first step. Make sure to give everything a go by yourself first however!

5.2 Abandon Perfection

Throughout GSCEs and A Levels, it would have been possible to answer all the questions with time to spare. This is not the case anymore. From now on, everything will be designed to stretch your mathematical ability. Most of the learning will happen through struggling with problems. It is worth remembering this when you feel like you've wasted hours on a question without making any progress: you will have been far more productive than it seems! Any time spent thinking about maths is time well spent.

It is worth noting that it can often be tempting to stay up all night, stop exercising and become an antisocial hermit to get an example sheet done. Everyone tries this once or twice (and usually realises that sheets are a lot harder the more tired you are!) However, this strategy is unsustainable. Remember, treat term-time like a marathon, not a sprint. There is always another sheet around the corner. Sometimes, moving on from questions you haven't been able to solve yet can be essential to ensure you are primed and ready for the next sheet. It is still manageable to catch-up on a few questions from different courses later on when you find time, but almost impossible to do the whole example sheet that you missed because you were exhausted from pulling an all-nighter.

5.3 Be Organised

Having good time management will make your life many orders of magnitude easier. Not only will you have more time to complete your studies, but you'll be able to enjoy yourself a lot more! It's key to know when your lectures, examples classes, supervisions and deadlines are, so try to keep up with it all (hint: add everything into a calendar so you can see it all in one place). Try not to leave an example sheet to the day before - trust us, this is not a fun experience. There are hundreds of interesting things to do in Cambridge, so try not to let the stress of last minute deadlines take over your life. If you are struggling with a piece of work, you can email your supervisors in advance and they will be understanding and try to help you get back on track. Make sure you schedule in time for hobbies and relaxation - and protect this time. Having time away from your problem sheets will not only help you find a solution when you've been stuck, but it is also essential to your well-being.

5.4 Create a System

For some people, creating a framework from which to learn your content is the most essential thing to develop whilst doing your degree. This should allow you to learn and understand content, easily access the relevant mathematical information when doing your work and revise content closer to exams. This is an example workflow:

  1. Check calendar, add any new deadlines/events and block out time for supervision work.
  2. e-read last lecture's notes before the lecture starts (lectures start at 10, so there is an opportunity beforehand to get some work done).
  3. Take notes clearly, copying exactly what the lecturer writes.
  4. Get back from lectures, go through notes by highlighting key definitions, theorems and proofs, methods and examples, etc.
  5. Try to answer any exercises from the lectures.
  6. Attempt examples sheets.
  7. Note down any questions or problems you're stuck on.
  8. Write up example sheet neatly. Hand in work by the deadline.
  9. Go to class, take notes onto copy of example sheet solutions.
  10. Discuss with supervision partner what you both want to cover in the supervision.
  11. Go to the supervision, ask questions from the sheet or lectures.

Note that this is definitely aspirational - very rarely does every part work. You must adapt and forgive yourself when you fall behind. You probably won't get your systems right straight away!

6 Social

Queens' has many opportunities for subject-based social events. Here's some to look out for in the first couple terms:

  • Queens' Maths Society - Student-run society attended by undergraduates, graduates and fellows. Hosts speakers fortnightly that talk about their mathematical research throughout Michaelmas and Lent terms. Often provides snacks.
  • Maths Mahal - A curry night for the undergraduate mathmos early in Michaelmas term to help people get to know each other. Gowns are mandatory at this event.
  • Maths Dinner - A formal dinner (formal wear but no gowns) in Old Hall for all Queens' Mathematicians in Lent term.

And don't forget to socialise outside of maths! There will be over four thousand people in your year in Cambridge, only three hundred of whom will be mathmos. So join some ensembles or orchestras, play some sports, hang out with students from other subjects, or pick up some activity you would never have previously considered on impulse!

7 When Things Go Wrong...

It is an uncomfortable truth, but there will almost certainly come a time when everything suddenly goes south. Perhaps it's a prolonged illness, a sudden emergency, or simply a time mismanagement that has grown out of control (or, if you're really unlucky, some combination at once or in quick succession). The first and most basic thing to do is to let people know and ask for help - and making sure you are keeping your DoS and tutor in the loop. They, and your supervisors, are fully on your side now that you are in the college and will quite happily move mountains to help you get back on track: they can (and will!) move around deadlines, let you know what questions on examples sheets to prioritise and which to save for a later date, and accommodate your needs however they can.

Also do not hesitate to reach out to those around you. Everyone else in the cohort is with you in the same boat and will be there to help you however they can if you need it. On the flip side, watch out for other people in your cohort and be there for them.

Cambridge is also an incredibly intense environment, and it is very possible to crack under the strain. If this ever happens to you,as above, keep in touch with your DoS and tutor and don't hesitate to reach out to your peers. But there is also the health and well being center along with the JCR (the student undergraduate body) welfare officers who are there to support and help you.

No matter what course of action you personally decide to go with, know that you are never alone during your time here.

8 Summary

So to sum it all up, you'll watch lectures, then you will get an example sheet, likely published online (at either at the DAMTP website for applied courses, or at the DPMMS website for pure courses). You will then attempt the questions and hand in neat solutions to your supervisor based on a deadline they set. Pay attention to your supervisors as some may want solutions handed in physically, but most will want it emailed to them. This may change throughout the year as well.

Then you'll attend an example class (at this point you may have your work returned to you). Finally, you'll have a supervision on that sheet. Talk to people. Develop a system to stay organised and stay sane. Be nice to yourself. Keep up your hobbies, and keep seeing your friends. Repeat that a few times. And most importantly, finish term happy!

9 Finally

Impostor Syndrome means feeling like you are not worthy of your place, feeling like an admissions error. Everyone here has felt this at some point in their Cambridge career but it is simply not true. You have been hand-picked from a crowded field of applicants based on the talent and the potential that the admissions tutors saw in you. They do not make mistakes. Bear that in mind when it feels like everyone else is coping better than you (pro tip: they will feel exactly the same way as you). There are plenty of friendly people to talk to around college about this (e.g. us or your college parents) so don't hesitate to ask. We were in the same boat a year ago and we are still in the same boat today. There is a variety of options for support, through your friends and peers, through the college welfare system and through the university. You are not struggling alone!

Have an amazing year, be sure to make the most of every opportunity and we can't wait to meet you!
